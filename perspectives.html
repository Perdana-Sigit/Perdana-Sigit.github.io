ETS-2 After Postponement: The Economic Questions That Remain

A delayed carbon market changes the timetable, but does it change the distribution of costs or the location of economic adjustment?

By Sigit Perdana · Approx. 5 min read

Central argument A later launch may ease short-term pressure, but the economic incidence of carbon pricing still depends on household constraints, regional infrastructure, and national climate obligations.

The European Union has postponed the full operation of its second emissions trading system (ETS-2) from 2027 to 2028. For many households, that decision may seem straightforward: an additional year without a new carbon cost on heating and road fuels. Yet postponement is not the same as removing the economic pressures that motivated the policy. Buildings and transport still require emissions reductions, fuel dependence remains uneven, and Member States continue to face binding obligations under the Effort Sharing Regulation (ESR). The central question is therefore not simply whether the delay is welcome, but what it changes in the allocation of costs, incentives, and adjustment over time.

A different timetable, not an empty policy framework

ETS-2 places compliance obligations on fuel suppliers rather than directly on households or motorists. Those suppliers are expected to pass through some of the allowance cost, though the extent and timing of pass-through will depend on market conditions. The amended European Climate Law postpones full ETS-2 operation to 2028, while early allowance auctions are scheduled for 2027. The institutions have also strengthened the market stability reserve to help address excessive volatility. These safeguards matter, but a mechanism that releases allowances when a price threshold is crossed is not a guaranteed ceiling on the price ultimately paid by consumers.

One year can reduce near-term political and financial pressure, especially during a period of concern about living costs. It can also create breathing room for governments to improve compensation and low-carbon alternatives. But a later start can compress the period available for households and firms to respond before the 2030 horizon. If investment in insulation, heating systems, charging infrastructure, and public transport is also postponed, the underlying dependence on fossil fuels will remain. Whether the delay eases adjustment or merely shifts it into a shorter window depends on what happens during the intervening period.

The distributional question is more than income

Carbon pricing is often assessed through national-average household costs or income deciles. These indicators are useful but incomplete. A low-income household in a poorly insulated home may have little scope to reduce heating demand. A rural commuter without reliable transport alternatives cannot respond to a fuel price increase in the same way as an urban resident with accessible public transport. Family composition, dwelling characteristics, and the location of jobs affect exposure as much as income alone.

The distributional consequences of carbon pricing depend not only on household income but also on where people live, how they heat their homes, and the alternatives available for everyday mobility. Regional economic structures and existing infrastructure can amplify these differences, leaving some households and territories more exposed than others. Evidence from quantitative assessments of ETS-2 illustrates how a common carbon price can translate into substantially different welfare effects. These findings are scenario-based rather than predictions of the precise costs households will face in 2028. Nevertheless, they highlight a fundamental economic issue: postponing carbon pricing does not, by itself, eliminate the structural conditions that make its consequences uneven.

This distinction also matters for the Social Climate Fund. Its effectiveness should not be judged solely by the amount of financial compensation distributed, but also by whether it helps households reduce their dependence on fossil fuels. Temporary income support may cushion immediate costs, while investments in building renovation, cleaner heating, and affordable transport can reduce exposure to future energy-price increases. Effective targeting therefore requires attention to regional infrastructure and household circumstances, not simply national income thresholds.

ETS-2 and ESR: two different layers of responsibility

Delaying the new carbon market does not, by itself, suspend the national emissions commitments already in place. Postponement changes the timing of one policy instrument, not the broader obligations under which Member States must act.

The interaction between ETS-2 and the Effort Sharing Regulation (ESR) adds another layer of complexity. While ETS-2 introduces a common carbon-price signal for covered activities, the ESR establishes binding national emissions targets. These two instruments operate through different mechanisms, and their economic implications should not be conflated. In particular, the distribution of financial obligations does not necessarily correspond to where actual adjustments in production, energy consumption, and emissions take place. This distinction matters when assessing how responsibilities and economic burdens are shared among Member States.

The useful test of postponement, then, is not whether it eliminates an unpopular price. It is whether the additional time changes households' ability to respond, the readiness of supporting infrastructure, and the coherence between national obligations and EU-wide incentives. Those economic outcomes, rather than the calendar alone, will determine how the costs of the transition are experienced.

Perspective 02 · Policy analysis 28 October 2026

CBAM Beyond Carbon Leakage: Trade, Competitiveness, and Welfare

Europe’s border carbon measure affects far more than the carbon content of imports. Its effects travel through trade, production, and household welfare.

By Sigit Perdana · Approx. 5 min read

Central argument A measure can improve the competitiveness of some exporters without increasing national welfare, and trade diversion is not the same as global emissions reduction.

The European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) entered its definitive regime on 1 January 2026. Its central rationale is familiar: if European producers face a carbon price while imports do not, production and emissions may shift to jurisdictions with weaker constraints. CBAM seeks to reduce that risk by attaching a carbon-related obligation to imports of covered goods. But whether this achieves its intended environmental purpose cannot be established simply by observing import prices, trade flows, or revenues. A border measure travels through an interconnected economic system, creating adjustments that can differ sharply across countries, industries, and households.

A carbon price at the border is not a single economic outcome

In its current form, CBAM covers selected carbon-intensive goods including iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, electricity, and hydrogen. The compliance system is being introduced alongside the gradual reduction of free allowances for relevant sectors under the EU Emissions Trading System. A simplification adopted in 2025 introduced a 50-tonne annual mass threshold for many smaller importers. While obligations apply to imports made in 2026, certificate purchases on the central platform begin in February 2027, with the first annual compliance deadline in September 2027. These implementation details matter because incentives depend on the timing of cash payments, rules for embedded emissions, and recognition of carbon prices already paid abroad.

The mechanism may strengthen the position of relatively low-emission producers serving the EU market. It may also encourage some foreign firms to lower the carbon intensity of production. Neither effect should be confused with a guaranteed reduction in global emissions. Producers can redirect cleaner output towards Europe while supplying more carbon-intensive output elsewhere, a response often described as resource shuffling. Changes in sourcing and trade routes can likewise shift emissions rather than eliminate them. Environmental effectiveness therefore depends on production decisions across markets, not only on emissions embodied in the goods entering the EU.

Competitiveness gains are not welfare gains

Research on CBAM's implications for Brazil illustrates a particularly important distinction. Brazil's comparatively low-carbon production in certain energy-intensive industries can confer a competitive advantage under an EU border adjustment. Yet improvements in sectoral trade performance need not translate into higher welfare for the whole Brazilian economy. Changes in international prices, resource allocation, and household consumption can offset gains accruing to particular exporters. A country can therefore look like a 'winner' in one trade indicator while experiencing losses in a broader economic measure.

The same divergence appears when considering least developed countries. Exempting vulnerable trading partners may reduce their direct economic burden, but it can weaken incentives against carbon leakage. In related modelling, alternative uses of CBAM revenue, including support for renewable energy and energy efficiency, can produce a different combination of welfare and environmental outcomes. These are policy scenarios rather than measures already adopted by the EU. Their significance is analytical: a border charge, an exemption, and a targeted investment programme affect behaviour through different channels, even when presented as responses to the same concern.

Strategic responses are another important part of the story. Research on China considers retaliation, changes in the composition of exports, and resource shuffling. Such behaviour reflects firms' and governments' attempts to protect competitiveness within the constraints they face. A change that benefits an individual exporter or trading partner does not necessarily improve aggregate emissions, and a more stringent border rule does not automatically create the strongest incentive for decarbonisation everywhere. These questions require analysis of general-equilibrium adjustments rather than an assumption that import substitution is equivalent to an emissions reduction.

The next test is policy performance, not policy symbolism

The practical debate is already moving beyond initial implementation. EU institutions are considering extending CBAM to selected downstream products and strengthening anti-circumvention measures; as of October 2026, that extension remains a legislative proposal rather than an adopted expansion. The concern is economically plausible: a measure applied to basic materials can shift incentives towards importing products further along the value chain. Yet broadening coverage also changes compliance costs and trade exposure, so the net consequences deserve careful assessment.

A rigorous evaluation should therefore ask several questions at once. Does CBAM lower global emissions or merely alter where clean and carbon-intensive output is sold? Who captures the competitiveness benefits, and who bears adjustment costs? How do responses differ between large industrial economies and exporters with limited technological or administrative capacity? What happens to households and downstream industries when input costs change? These questions do not presuppose that CBAM is either a success or a failure. They recognise that its economic effects cannot be read directly from its stated objective.

The most useful contribution of economic analysis is to reveal these mechanisms and trade-offs, including outcomes that do not fit a simple narrative of winners and losers. The climate case for a border adjustment ultimately rests on what happens to emissions and production across the wider economy, not merely on the existence of a carbon charge at Europe's frontier.
